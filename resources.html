What is implicit bias?

Implicit bias is the unconscious assumption/ belief about a group (gender, age, race, etc..), that affects how you understand and make decisions. Source: Project Implicit.

Imagine this: A group needs someone to handle the technical work. You immediately choose a man before asking anyone about their experience. That decision could reflect an assumption about gender and ability.

Our thoughts on implicit bias

We connected the readings to the what we learned while growing up. The idea of Implicit Bias makes us question why people judge another based on their appearance, especially the judgement upon POC. However through our experiences, the idea differs and we questioned whether the readings and tests perfectly match reality.

What is intersectionality?

Kimberlé Crenshaw developed intersectionality to examine how systems such as racism and sexism overlap. Looking at race or gender separately can miss the experiences of people affected by both. Source: Columbia Law School.

Imagine: a student who has a disability and a limited budget. An online course might offer accessible software but require an expensive computer. Addressing accessibility alone would leave the cost barrier in place.

Our thoughts on intersectionality

Casey: Intersectionality is the overlapping between different parts of someone’s identity and experiences. Looking at someone from the outside does not tell us everything about how they experience society or technology.

Ethan: These overlapping identities matter, but this also raises the concern about how the term is used. I’d rather people explain the specific situation than rely on a broad label. My discussion with Casey solidified two priorities: recognizing how identities interact and being clear about the experiences we are describing.

Put these ideas into practice

  • Before assigning group roles, ask everyone about their interests and experience.
  • Use the same clear criteria when comparing people’s work.
  • Ask who might face barriers involving cost, language, accessibility, or time.
  • Listen when someone describes an experience different from yours.
  • Review outcomes as well as intentions: who actually gets access or opportunities?
