What is implicit bias?

Implicit bias refers to automatic associations that can shape how we see people without our awareness. These associations can come from repeated messages and experiences. Explicit bias, in comparison, involves attitudes we consciously recognize or express. Source: Project Implicit.

Imagine this: A group needs someone to handle the technical work. You immediately choose a man before asking anyone about their experience. That decision could reflect an assumption about gender and ability. This hypothetical example illustrates a possibility; it does not prove what caused any particular decision.

Our thoughts on implicit bias

Casey connected the readings and the test to associations learned while growing up. She questioned judging people based only on their appearance. Ethan brought a different perspective to the discussion of racism: people he knew had been willing to discuss it openly, which made him question how closely the reading matched his experience.

What does the IBT tell us?

The assignment calls this the Implicit Bias Test (IBT). Project Implicit’s specific task is called the Implicit Association Test (IAT). It compares how quickly someone sorts categories when they are paired in different ways, using differences in response time to estimate relative automatic associations.

A result is a starting point for reflection. It does not diagnose someone’s character or reliably predict a particular act of discrimination. Scores can vary across occasions, and reliability and interpretation have limitations. Source: About the IAT.

Our thoughts on the implicit bias test

The test made us notice how quickly we categorize information. Casey reflected on how our experiences and surroundings can shape associations without us consciously recognizing them. Ethan noticed how easily we separated words into categories such as male, female, work, and family. What stood out in our discussion was the process itself, rather than a specific test score. It gave us a reason to pause and think about where our automatic associations come from.

Explore Project Implicit’s tests. Review the participation information and age requirements first. Participation and sharing personal results should be voluntary.

What is intersectionality?

Kimberlé Crenshaw developed intersectionality to examine how systems such as racism and sexism overlap. Looking at race or gender separately can miss the experiences of people affected by both. Source: Columbia Law School.

On campus: Imagine a student who has a disability and a limited budget. An online course might offer accessible software but require an expensive computer. Addressing accessibility alone would leave the cost barrier in place. This is an illustrative example of why overlapping circumstances matter.

Our thoughts on intersectionality

Casey understood intersectionality as the overlap between different parts of someone’s identity and experiences. Looking at someone from the outside does not tell us everything about how they experience society or technology.

Ethan agreed that these overlapping identities matter, but also raised a concern about how the term is used. He wanted people to explain the specific situation rather than rely on a broad label. Our discussion brought together two priorities: recognizing how identities interact and being clear about the experiences we are describing.

Put these ideas into practice

  • Before assigning group roles, ask everyone about their interests and experience.
  • Use the same clear criteria when comparing people’s work.
  • Ask who might face barriers involving cost, language, accessibility, or time.
  • Listen when someone describes an experience different from yours.
  • Review outcomes as well as intentions: who actually gets access or opportunities?

A question to take with you

When did you last make a quick assumption about someone? What information would have helped you make a more thoughtful decision?
